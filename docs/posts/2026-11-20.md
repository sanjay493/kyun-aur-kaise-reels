## v051 Fan on, room still hot. Why do you feel cool?
Series: Science Around Us · Class 9 (Matter in Our Surroundings: evaporation) · 22.0 s · 67 words

Voiceover:
Fan on, but the room isn't any cooler. So why do you feel cooler? On a hot day, your skin has a thin layer of sweat. As sweat evaporates, it takes heat from your body. The fan blows the damp air away, so sweat evaporates faster. On sticky, humid days, the air is already full of moisture, so the fan helps less. Fans cool people, not rooms.

Caption:
Evaporation needs heat. When sweat turns into vapour, it takes that heat (the latent heat of vaporisation) from your skin, and you feel cooler 🌀
Your Class 9 book lists what speeds up evaporation: higher temperature, more surface area, lower humidity and more wind. A fan adds the wind.
That's also why cotton clothes feel better in summer: cotton soaks up sweat and spreads it out so it can evaporate.
Since a fan cools you and not the room, switching it off when nobody is there saves electricity without making anyone hotter.

## v052 3 cm, 4 cm, 8 cm: a triangle?
Series: Maths Without Fear · Class 7 (Ganita Prakash, A Tale of Three Intersecting Lines: triangle inequality) · 24.2 s · 68 words

Voiceover:
Three sticks: three, four and eight centimetres. Can they make a triangle? Lay the long one down. Raise the short ones from its ends. Even stretched flat, three plus four is only seven. They can't meet. So the rule: any two sides together must be longer than the third. Quick check: add the two shorter sides. Six plus six beats seven. Triangle! Short sides too short? No triangle.

Caption:
Why is checking only the two shorter sides enough? If the two shortest add up to more than the longest, every other pair is bigger still, so they will be more than the third side too 📐
Think of it as paths: going straight from one corner to another is always shorter than taking a detour through the third corner.
Try these with ice-cream sticks or straws: 5, 5, 9? 2, 3, 5? 4, 7, 12? (Answers: yes; no, 2 + 3 only equals 5, so the sticks lie flat; no)

## v053 Upset? Put it in one word
Series: Teen Talk · all classes, parents · 23.4 s · 66 words

Voiceover:
Angry after a fight? Try putting the feeling into one word. In a U.C.L.A. study, people saw photos of angry or scared faces. Choosing a word for the feeling lowered activity in the amygdala, an emotion area. A thinking area behind the forehead got busier. Like hitting the brakes on emotions. Talking or writing it down may help the same way. Name it to tame it.

Caption:
In the 2007 UCLA study, 30 adults looked at faces showing emotions inside a brain scanner. Picking the word "angry" or "fearful" for a face lowered amygdala activity; picking a name like "Harry" for the same face didn't 🧠
The researcher, Matthew Lieberman, compared it to hitting the brakes when you see a yellow light, and said the effect is likely to be modest. It won't fix a problem, but it can give you a calmer moment to think.
Try a more exact word than "bad": hurt, embarrassed, left out, nervous, frustrated.
If strong feelings keep getting in the way of daily life, talk to a parent, teacher or school counsellor.
#TeenTalk #Emotions #MentalHealthMatters #FeelingsMatter #Parenting

Sources: UCLA Health news release, "Putting feelings into words produces therapeutic effects in the brain" (21 Jun 2007; study in Psychological Science; 30 participants; emotion words "angry" and "fearful" vs names "Harry" and "Sally"; "When you attach the word 'angry,' you see a decreased response in the amygdala"; the right ventrolateral prefrontal cortex, "located behind the forehead and eyes", was more active; "you seem to be hitting the brakes on your emotional responses"; talking with a friend or writing in a journal "may have benefits"; "These effects are likely to be modest") https://www.uclahealth.org/news/putting-feelings-into-words-produces-therapeutic-effects-in-the-brain-ucla-neuroimaging-study-supports-ancient-buddhist-teachings

## v054 Face down, on silent, still stealing focus
Series: Study Smart · all classes · 24.8 s · 67 words

Voiceover:
Your phone is on the desk, face down, on silent. Harmless, right? Researchers in Texas gave nearly eight hundred people concentration tests. Some kept their phones on the desk, some in a pocket or bag, and some in another room. The other-room group did best, clearly beating the desk group. Even silent, a nearby phone quietly uses up some of your attention. Studying? Phone in another room.

Caption:
The 2017 University of Texas at Austin study found it didn't matter whether the phone was on or off, or face up or face down. Just having it nearby took some of people's brainpower 📵
Researcher Adrian Ward called it a "brain drain": trying not to think about your phone uses up some of your limited attention.
Studying at home? Put the phone in another room (or ask someone to keep it) for a 30-minute block, then check it on a break.
#StudySmart #StudyTips #FocusTips #ExamPreparation #BoardExams

It's a brain drain.") https://news.utexas.edu/2017/06/26/the-mere-presence-of-your-smartphone-reduces-brain-power
