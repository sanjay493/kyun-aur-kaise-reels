## v067 Thank-you notes feel better than you think
Series: Teen Talk · all classes, parents, teachers · 25.7 s · 70 words

Voiceover:
Want to thank someone, but it feels awkward? Here's a surprise. In a University of Chicago study, people wrote thank-you letters to someone who helped them. Writers guessed how the reader would feel. Then the readers were asked. Readers felt happier and more surprised than the writers expected, and less awkward. Writers worried about the right words. Readers just felt the warmth. Don't wait for perfect words. Say thank you.

Caption:
In the 2018 study by Amit Kumar and Nicholas Epley, people wrote short letters of gratitude. Writers underestimated how happy and surprised the readers would feel, and overestimated how awkward it would be 💌
The researchers think one reason we hold back is that we worry about saying it well, while the person reading mostly notices the warmth.
Try it this week: a two-line message to a teacher, a friend, a cousin or the didi/bhaiya who helped you. Say what they did and how it helped.
#TeenTalk #Gratitude #Kindness #Friendship #Parenting

Sources: University of Chicago News, "People underestimate value of sending letters of appreciation" (5 Sep 2018; "a series of four experiments"; "Expressers significantly underestimated how surprised recipients would be about why expressers were grateful, overestimated how awkward recipients would feel, and underestimated how positive recipients would feel"; "While the writers worried about choosing the right words, the recipients were happy simply by the warmth of the gesture"; Kumar & Epley, Psychological Science, 2018) https://news.uchicago.edu/story/people-underestimate-value-sending-letters-appreciation ;
Association for Psychological Science, "People Underestimate the Power of Saying 'Thanks'" (13 Jul 2018; "recipients reported even more surprise and delight than what the senders expected"; "expressers overestimated the awkwardness that the recipient would feel") https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/observer/obsonline/people-underestimate-the-power-of-saying-thanks.html

## v068 Why does February get a 29th day?
Series: Science Around Us · Class 6 (The Earth Our Habitat: Motions of the Earth) · 23.3 s · 69 words

Voiceover:
Why does February sometimes get a twenty ninth day? Earth takes about three hundred sixty five and a quarter days to circle the Sun. Our calendar year has three hundred sixty five, so six hours are left over. In four years, that's twenty four hours. One whole day. So every fourth year, February gets day twenty nine. A leap year. Six hours a year, saved up into one day.

Caption:
A leap year has 366 days instead of 365. The next one is 2028 📅
Bonus rule: adding a day every four years adds a little too much over time, so most century years skip it. 1900 was not a leap year, but 2000 was (every 400 years the leap day is kept).
Quick check: which of these are leap years? 2024, 2026, 2100, 2400. (Answers: 2024 yes; 2026 no; 2100 no; 2400 yes)
Born on 29 February? You get a real birthday only once every four years!

## v069 Why is a circle's area πr²?
Series: Maths Without Fear · Class 7 (Perimeter and area: area of a circle) · 24.4 s · 69 words

Voiceover:
Why is a circle's area pi r squared? Cut a pizza and find out. Slice it into many equal pieces, and lay them tips up, tips down. It looks almost like a rectangle. The more slices, the straighter it gets. Height: the radius, r. Length: half the crust, which is pi r. Rectangle area: length times height. Pi r times r. Pi r squared. Every circle hides a rectangle.

Caption:
The crust is the circle's circumference, 2πr. Half the slices point up and half point down, so each long side of the "rectangle" gets half the crust: ½ × 2πr = πr 🍕
With 12 slices the edges are still bumpy. With 100 or 1,000 thinner slices the shape gets closer and closer to a perfect rectangle, so the area becomes exactly πr².
Try it with a paper circle: fold it in half 3 times, cut along the folds into 8 pieces and arrange them tips up, tips down.

Sources: Math is Fun, "Area of a Circle by Cutting into Sectors" ("Cut a circle into equal sectors"; "The height is the circle's radius"; the width "is about half the circumference of the circle"; "Half the Circumference = π × radius"; "Area = (π × radius) × (radius) = π × radius2"; "the more we divide the circle up, the closer we get to being exactly right") https://www.mathsisfun.com/geometry/circle-area-by-sectors.html ;
BYJU'S ("The rearranged figure can be visualised as a rectangle with base equal to half the circumference of the circle (πr)" and "the width as the radius (r)"; "The area of the rectangle is equal to πr×r=πr2") https://byjus.com/question-answer/the-area-of-a-circle-can-be-visualised-by-dividing-the-circle-into-equal-sectors-40

## v070 Lose or loose?
Series: English Made Easy · all classes · 22.8 s · 70 words

Voiceover:
Don't loose your phone. Spot the mistake? Lose, with one O, is a verb. To lose your phone, or lose a match. Loose, with two O's, usually means not tight. A loose shirt, a loose tooth. They sound different too. Lose rhymes with choose. Loose rhymes with goose. Trick: loose has an extra O, like the extra room in a loose shirt. Lost something? One O. Not tight? Two O's.

Caption:
Lose (one O) is a verb: lose a pen, lose a match, lose your temper. Loose (two Os) is usually an adjective: a loose shirt, a loose screw, loose change ✍️
Hear the difference: lose has a Z sound (like choose); loose has an S sound (like goose).
Want a verb from loose? Use loosen: "Loosen your tie."
Try these: Don't ___ hope. My shoelace is ___. We might ___ the final. This button is ___. (Answers: lose, loose, lose, loose)
