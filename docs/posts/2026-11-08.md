## v035 Is 0.999... less than 1?
Series: Maths Without Fear · Class 9 (Number Systems) · 24.4 s · 70 words

Voiceover:
Point nine nine nine, with nines forever. Is it just less than one? Call it x. Ten x is nine point nine nine nine, forever. Subtract x. The endless nines cancel, so nine x is nine. So x is exactly one. Not almost. Check: one third is point three three three, forever. Times three: point nine nine nine. And three thirds is one. Two ways to write the same number.

Caption:
Another way to see it: if 0.999... were smaller than 1, there would be a number in between. Try to find one. You can't 🤯
This is straight from NCERT Class 9, Number Systems, Exercise 1.3: "Express 0.99999... in the form p/q. Are you surprised by your answer?"
The same trick turns any recurring decimal into a fraction: x = 0.777..., 10x = 7.777..., so 9x = 7 and x = 7/9.

## v036 How does one spoon turn milk into curd?
Series: Science Around Us · Class 8 (Microorganisms: Friend and Foe) · 24.9 s · 70 words

Voiceover:
How does one spoon of curd turn a whole bowl of milk into curd? That spoon carries bacteria called Lactobacillus. In warm milk, they multiply. They feed on lactose, the sugar in milk, and turn it into lactic acid. The acid makes milk proteins clump together. Runny milk sets thick. Too cold or too hot, it won't set well. So we use warm milk. Dahi is milk, changed by bacteria.

Caption:
That spoon of yesterday's dahi is called the starter. It carries the bacteria that do the work overnight 🥛
Milk left out without a starter can also turn thick, but that is usually spoiled milk, not set curd.
Try it: set two small bowls with the same starter, one in a warm spot and one in the fridge. Check which one sets.
Class 8 Science, Microorganisms: Friend and Foe.

## v037 Nervous before speaking? Try this
Series: Teen Talk · all classes, parents, teachers · 25.5 s · 67 words

Voiceover:
Heart racing before you speak in assembly? Everyone says, calm down. But nervousness and excitement feel alike: fast heartbeat, high energy. In a Harvard study, people who said I'm excited before a speech were rated more persuasive than those who said I'm calm. Turning nerves into excitement may be easier than forcing calm. So next time, say it out loud: I'm excited. Same racing heart, new name.

Caption:
Nervous and excited are both "high-arousal" feelings: racing heart, lots of energy. That's why the researcher suggests it may be easier to switch to excited than to force yourself calm 🎤
In the same study, people who reframed their nerves as excitement also did better on hard maths problems and a karaoke singing task.
Try it before: a class presentation, a debate, a viva, singing at the annual function.
If worry stays for days or stops you from doing things you enjoy, talk to a parent, teacher or school counsellor.
#TeenTalk #StageFear #PublicSpeaking #ConfidenceTips #StudentLife

Sources: American Psychological Association news release via EurekAlert, "Getting excited helps with performance anxiety more than trying to calm down, study finds" (23 Dec 2013), on Alison Wood Brooks (Harvard Business School), "Get Excited: Reappraising Pre-Performance Anxiety as Excitement", Journal of Experimental Psychology: General (participants said "I am excited" or "I am calm"; excited-group speeches rated "more persuasive, competent and relaxed"; excited group scored 8% higher on maths; karaoke scores higher for "excited"; "both anxiety and excitement are emotional states characterized by high arousal"; "it may be easier to view anxiety as excitement rather than trying to calm down") https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/607078

## v038 Is ₹100 in your gullak still ₹100?
Series: Money Smart (new) · all classes, parents · 24.8 s · 69 words

Voiceover:
Keep a hundred rupee note in your gullak for ten years. Is it still worth a hundred? The note still says one hundred. But prices usually rise every year. That rise in prices over time is called inflation. So the same note buys less. That's called losing purchasing power. If prices rise six percent a year, they about double in twelve years. Money kept still doesn't grow. Prices do.

Caption:
Quick estimate, the "rule of 72": divide 72 by the yearly price rise to see roughly how many years prices take to double. 72 ÷ 6 = 12 years 📈
India's target: the Government has kept the RBI's retail inflation target at 4%, with a band of 2% to 6%, for April 2026 to March 2031. Even at 4% a year, prices roughly double in about 18 years.
Ask at home: what did a cinema ticket or a plate of samosas cost when your parents were your age?
