## v083 Why is a rainbow always opposite the Sun?
Series: Science Around Us · Class 10 (Ch. 10 "The Human Eye and the Colourful World", 10.4) · 23.5 s · 68 words

Voiceover:
Want to see a rainbow? Turn your back to the Sun. After rain, tiny water droplets float in the air. Each droplet acts like a small prism, splitting sunlight into colours. Light bends going in, reflects off the back, and bends again coming out. So the colours head back towards the Sun's side, where you stand. A rainbow is always opposite the Sun. Sun behind, rain in front.

Caption:
Red is always on the outside of the arch and violet on the inside 🌈 The rainbow sits about 42° from the point exactly opposite the Sun, around your head's shadow.
Sometimes you can spot a fainter second rainbow outside the first. Its light reflects twice inside each drop instead of once.
Rainbows aren't only for rainy days: look at a waterfall, a fountain or garden-hose spray with the Sun behind you.

## v084 A perfect right angle from a rope: 3, 4, 5
Series: Maths Without Fear · Class 7–8 (Triangles / Baudhayana-Pythagoras theorem) · 23.6 s · 65 words

Voiceover:
How do you make a perfect right angle with a rope? Mark it with knots at three, four and five units. Pull it into a triangle. The corner between three and four: ninety degrees. Why? Three squared plus four squared: nine plus sixteen, twenty-five. Five squared! If two sides' squares add up to the third's square, it's a right angle. Three, four, five: a right angle from a rope.

Caption:
Try it with a string: mark 12 equal parts, then pin it down as a triangle with sides of 3, 4 and 5 parts 📐 Check the corner with your set square.
Other sets that work: 6, 8, 10 (36 + 64 = 100) and 5, 12, 13 (25 + 144 = 169). Whole-number sets like these are called Pythagorean triples.
In India this property is also linked to Baudhayana, whose Śulba-Sūtras describe constructions with cords.
Quick check: do 7, 24, 25 make a right angle?

## v085 Forwarded many times? Pause before you share
Series: Smart & Safe · all classes, parents · 24.0 s · 67 words

Voiceover:
Seen two arrows and the words, forwarded many times, on a WhatsApp message? Pause. It means the message has passed through a chain of five or more chats. It didn't start with whoever sent it to you. WhatsApp itself says: being shared many times doesn't make it true. Photos and videos can be edited. Check trusted news sites first. Many forwards don't mean more truth. Check before you share.

Caption:
A single "Forwarded" label also means the message was first written by someone else, not the person who sent it 📲
Since April 2020, a "forwarded many times" message can only be forwarded to one chat at a time.
Quick checks from WhatsApp's own help page:
• Stories that seem hard to believe are often untrue.
• Spelling mistakes, or a request for personal information, are warning signs.
• Is it reported by several trustworthy sources?
News about the Government of India? Send it to PIB Fact Check on WhatsApp: +91 87997 11259. It's free.

## v086 Why ₹199 feels so much cheaper than ₹200
Series: Money Smart · all classes, parents · 27.8 s · 69 words

Voiceover:
Why does ₹199 feel so much cheaper than ₹200? It's just one rupee less. We read left to right, so the first digit hits first: one versus two. A 2005 study named this the left-digit effect. It only worked when the first digit changed. So when you see ₹499 or ₹999, round up in your head. ₹199 is really ₹200. Read the whole price, not just the first digit.

Caption:
In the study, students compared pen prices. $2.99 felt cheaper than $3.00, but $3.59 vs $3.60 made no real difference: the first digit didn't change 🏷️
The researchers' explanation: our brain starts judging a number's size before we've finished reading all its digits.
The effect was more likely when the two prices being compared were close.
Try it next time you shop: say the rounded price out loud. ₹1,499 → "about fifteen hundred."
#MoneySmart #FinancialLiteracy #SmartShopping #MoneyTips #Psychology

Sources: Thomas, M. & Morwitz, V. (2005), "Penny Wise and Pound Foolish: The Left-Digit Effect in Price Cognition", Journal of Consumer Research 32(1): 54–64, abstract ("nine-ending prices are perceived to be smaller than a price one cent higher"; "this occurs only when the leftmost digits of the prices differ (e.g., $2.99 vs. $3.00)"; "The closer the two prices being compared, the more likely is the left-digit effect") https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/v32y2005i1p54-64.html ;
NYU Stern Business (Fall 2005), "A Penny Saved" by the authors ("only if the left-most digit changes to a lower level (e.g., $3.00 to $2.99) but not if the left-most digit remains unchanged (e.g. $3.60 to $3.59)"; "Because we process data from left to right, the encoded magnitude of $2.99 could ... be significantly less than that of $3.00"; "encoding the magnitude of a multi-digit number begins even before we finish reading all the digits") https://w4.stern.nyu.edu/sternbusiness/fall_2005/pennySaved.html
