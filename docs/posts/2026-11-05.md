## v023 One of my friend or one of my friends?
Series: English Made Easy · all classes · 22.0 s · 68 words

Voiceover:
One of my friend is coming. Sounds right? Look again. One of means one from a group. A group has many friends, so the noun is plural. Friends. But you're talking about just one person, so the verb stays singular. Is. One of my friends is coming. One of the boys is late. Picture the group first, then pick one from it. One of many friends, one is.

Caption:
Two things to get right in every "one of" sentence ✍️ The noun after "one of" is plural (friends, boys, cities). The verb matches "one", so it's singular: is, was, has, lives.
Same pattern: "One of the best players in our team is from Ranchi."
Try it: "One of my (cousin / cousins) (live / lives) in Pune." Answer in the comments.

## v024 Same type of sum, 20 times in a row?
Series: Study Smart · all classes (maths practice) · 25.6 s · 65 words

Voiceover:
Twenty sums of one type in a row feels easy. That might be the problem. With blocked practice, you never decide which method to use. Mixing types makes you choose each time. That's called interleaving. In one study, seventh graders practised maths for nine weeks. On a surprise test, mixed topics scored seventy-two percent. Blocked topics, thirty-eight. Don't just practise the method. Practise choosing it.

Caption:
In exams, questions don't come labelled "this is the area chapter" 🧠 You first have to spot what kind of problem it is. Blocked practice skips that step; mixed practice trains it.
Try it in revision: make a set of 10 questions taken from 3 or 4 different chapters and shuffle them.
Honest note: this is one classroom study (140 Class 7 students, tested two weeks later). The researchers say spacing practice out may explain part of the gain too (see our earlier reel on spacing).
#StudySmart #StudyTips #CBSEMaths #ExamPreparation #IndianStudents

Sources: Rohrer, Dedrick & Burgess (2014), "The benefit of interleaved mathematics practice is not limited to superficially similar kinds of problems", Psychonomic Bulletin & Review 21, 1323–1330 (grade 7, n = 140; unannounced test two weeks after a nine-week practice phase; 72% interleaved vs 38% blocked; interleaving "requires that students choose an appropriate strategy for each problem") https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-014-0588-3 (full text read: https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2014-rohrer.pdf) ;

## v025 Add up odd numbers. Notice anything?
Series: Maths Without Fear · Class 6–8 (number patterns, square numbers) · 25.0 s · 70 words

Voiceover:
Add odd numbers, starting from one. Watch the answers. One plus three is four. Plus five, nine. Plus seven, sixteen. Four, nine, sixteen. All square numbers! Why? Each odd number adds an L-shaped layer of dots. One dot, plus three, makes two by two. Five more make three by three. So the first ten odd numbers add up to one hundred. Odd numbers build squares, one layer at a time.

Caption:
Why does it work? To grow an n × n square into an (n+1) × (n+1) square, you add one new row and one new column that share a corner: n + n + 1 = 2n + 1 dots. That's always the next odd number 🟨
So 1 + 3 + 5 + … + (2n − 1) = n².
Try it: add the first 15 odd numbers (1 + 3 + … + 29) without adding them one by one. Answer in the comments.

## v026 Seen, but no reply?
Series: Teen Talk · all classes, parents · 23.4 s · 69 words

Voiceover:
Your message shows seen, but no reply for hours. Are they angry with you? It's easy to jump to the worst story. But think of other reasons. Their phone died. They're in class. They're helping at home. Or they just forgot. Seen only means the message was opened, not what they feel. If it still bothers you, ask kindly. All okay? Don't read silence as anger. Ask, don't assume.

Caption:
Most of us have left a message on "seen" at some point, often for an ordinary reason 📱 Opening a message and having time to reply properly are two different things.
Works the other way too: when you can't reply, a quick "busy, will text later" saves your friend the guessing.
Parents: the same idea helps when a teen goes quiet. A calm "all okay?" opens more doors than "why didn't you answer?"
