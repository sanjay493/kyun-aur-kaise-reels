## v075 Why do a quadrilateral's angles add up to 360°?
Series: Maths Without Fear · Class 8 (Quadrilaterals: angle sum property) · 27.5 s · 69 words

Voiceover:
Draw any four-sided shape, even a crooked one. Its angles always add up to three hundred sixty degrees. Why? Draw one diagonal, corner to corner. Now you have two triangles. Each triangle's angles add up to one hundred eighty degrees. Two triangles: one hundred eighty plus one hundred eighty. Three hundred sixty! A five-sided shape? Three triangles, five hundred forty degrees. Stuck on a shape? Cut it into triangles.

Caption:
The general rule: a shape with n sides splits into (n − 2) triangles from one corner, so its angles add up to (n − 2) × 180° 📐
Hexagon? 6 − 2 = 4 triangles, so 4 × 180° = 720°.
Try it: draw a crooked quadrilateral in your notebook, measure all four angles with a protractor and add them. How close to 360° did you get?

## v076 Scared to ask for help?
Series: Teen Talk · all classes, parents, teachers · 22.3 s · 69 words

Voiceover:
Scared to ask for help? You might think people will say no. In Stanford studies, people asked strangers to lend a phone or fill in a form. They guessed they'd need to ask about fifty percent more people than needed. Why? For the person asked, saying no is harder than we think. Stuck on a sum? Ask your friend or teacher, clearly. More people say yes than you think.

Caption:
In the same research, people handing out a flyer expected more replies than people who asked face to face, but asking directly got more people to say yes 🙋
The researchers' tip: how you ask matters more than how big the favour is. A clear "Can you explain question 4 to me?" works better than a vague hint.
And next time someone asks you, remember how hard that ask might have felt for them.
#TeenTalk #AskForHelp #StudentLife #Confidence #Psychology

Sources: Stanford Graduate School of Business, "Francis Flynn: If You Want Something, Ask For It" (1 Jul 2008), on Flynn & Lake, "If you need help, just ask": Underestimating compliance with direct requests for help, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2008 ("people tend to grossly underestimate how likely others are to agree to requests for assistance"; borrowing strangers' cell phones, filling out questionnaires: "participants consistently overestimated by 50 percent the number of people they'd have to ask"; "Just saying no can make them look very bad"; "you should pay more attention to how your request is being made than to the size of your request") https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/francis-flynn-if-you-want-something-ask-it

## v077 Why does a cut apple turn brown?
Series: Science Around Us · all classes (everyday chemistry) · 26.9 s · 67 words

Voiceover:
Cut an apple and leave it. Soon the white part turns brown. Why? The knife breaks open apple cells. An enzyme inside, polyphenol oxidase, meets oxygen from the air. Their reaction makes brown pigments called melanins. It's called enzymatic browning. Bananas, potatoes and brinjals brown the same way. To slow it, add lemon juice, or keep the slices in the fridge. Broken cells plus air equals brown.

Caption:
Not all browning is bad: the same kind of reaction gives raisins, black tea, coffee and cocoa their dark colour 🍎
Different apple varieties have different amounts of the enzyme, so some brown faster than others.
Kitchen experiment: cut 4 slices. Leave one plain, and dip the others in lemon juice, pineapple juice and vinegar. Check them after an hour. Which stayed whitest?

## v078 Can you spot the fake link?
Series: Smart & Safe · all classes, parents · 25.7 s · 70 words

Voiceover:
Two website links. One is fake. Can you spot it? In the second, r and n sit together, like an m. Fake sites steal logins. India's cyber agency, C.E.R.T. In, says: check the spelling in every link. Don't tap unknown links in messages. Use the official app or website. Lost money? Call one nine three zero, or report at cybercrime dot gov dot in.

Caption:
More checks from CERT-In's 2025 safety booklet 🔍
• Look for HTTPS as well as spelling errors in the web address.
• Scanned a QR code? Check the link before opening it, and don't type bank details on a site a QR code opened.
• Banks and government offices don't ask for sensitive details by SMS.
• Download apps only from official app stores.
Share this with someone who pays bills online.
